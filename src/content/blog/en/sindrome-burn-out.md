# Burnout syndrome

Burnout, or the syndrome of "being burned out", is defined by the World Health Organization (WHO) as an occupational phenomenon resulting from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed. Although it is often confused with ordinary stress, burnout is a multidimensional exhaustion that covers the physical, emotional and mental spheres.

## Dimensions and symptoms

The syndrome shows up through three core pillars that affect a person's relationship with their work:

* **Emotional exhaustion:** A persistent feeling of lacking energy and emotional resources.
* **Cynicism or depersonalization:** Increased mental distance from one's job, along with feelings of negativity towards tasks or people in the workplace.
* **Reduced professional efficacy:** A decline in the ability to carry out work tasks and a negative assessment of one's own achievements.

Physically and mentally, symptoms include sleep problems (insomnia or hypersomnia), changes in appetite, severe fatigue, irritability, lack of concentration and feelings of uselessness. In severe cases it can lead to hypertension, heart disease, anxiety and depression.

## The close link with depression and anxiety

There is scientific debate over whether burnout is an independent condition or a type of depression, since they share a biological basis and symptoms such as anhedonia (loss of interest or pleasure). At the epigenetic level, it has been suggested that chronic stress in the work environment can cause chemical changes in DNA (methylation), acting as a biological marker of these disorders.

As for anxiety, people with higher levels of social anxiety or "trait anxiety" (a natural tendency to perceive situations as threatening) have been observed to be more likely to develop burnout. Emotional exhaustion and cynicism feed states of anxiety which, in turn, worsen the exhaustion.

## Causes, stages and risk factors

Burnout doesn't happen suddenly; it's a process that usually goes through four stages:

1. Enthusiasm and idealism stage.
2. Stagnation stage.
3. Resistance stage.
4. Total exhaustion stage.

The main causes are usually an overload of responsibilities, the perception of unfair treatment, isolation at work or a lack of well-being plans at the company. There are also two ways of coping with it: active burnout, where the person fights hard against the problem, and passive burnout, where interest in and connection with the organization is gradually lost.

## Risk groups and specific contexts

Burnout can coexist with neuropsychological conditions such as ADHD or autism, which complicates diagnosis due to the frustration caused by sensory hypersensitivity or the difficulty of fitting into complex social environments. Beyond the workplace, the syndrome can appear in caregivers, athletes, students and parents (parental burnout).

## Statistics and the work reality in Spain

According to a survey of 1,500 workers in Spain, the picture is worrying:

* 41% suffer from work-related stress.
* 55% admit to having felt completely exhausted at some point.
* 11% of employees say they are always stressed at work.
* This stress especially affects young people aged 25 to 44 (around 45% of them are regularly stressed), a stage of life marked by crucial decisions such as buying a home, starting a family or beginning a career.
* Although 24% have considered quitting their job for this reason, only 12% seek professional help, reflecting a cultural tendency to endure pressure in silence.

## Intervention and recovery strategies

To stop burnout from progressing and reverse its effects, it's essential to take an approach that combines physical health with restructuring daily habits. It's not just about "resting", but about making deep changes to your routine:

* **Sleep hygiene and disconnecting:** The first critical step is making sure you get quality rest. This means setting non-negotiable boundaries between personal and work life, avoiding the constant stream of digital notifications that keeps the brain on permanent alert.
* **Physical activity as a regulator:** Exercise isn't optional; it works as a biological tool to metabolize accumulated cortisol (the stress hormone).
* **Reorganizing your environment:** You need to identify and remove unnecessary "stressors", learning to delegate or to say "no" to responsibilities that exceed what you can realistically handle.
* **Professional support:** In advanced stages, where exhaustion has turned into anxiety or depression, psychotherapy and, sometimes, medication are essential to stabilize the nervous system.

## Diagnostic tools and warning signs

Early detection is key to avoiding a total collapse. To measure the severity of the syndrome, the [Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI)](https://estadisticando.blogspot.com/2016/04/escala-maslach-burnout-inventory-mbi.html) is used, an assessment system that scores three levels: the frequency and intensity of exhaustion, depersonalization and loss of personal accomplishment.

Beyond tests, there are very specific everyday indicators that work as warning signs:

* **Reactive procrastination:** Putting off extremely simple or routine tasks, not out of laziness, but because of a mental inability to process any more information.
* **Notification overload:** Feeling rejection or "micro-panic" at the sound of work emails or messages.
* **The exhaustion cycle:** There is a clear progression where initial stress (which can be motivating) crosses a red line into burnout, where performance plummets regardless of the effort put in.

These tools help put a name to what the worker is feeling, turning an abstract sense of discomfort into an actionable clinical diagnosis that makes it possible to start recovering.
